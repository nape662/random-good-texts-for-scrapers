## An update to "Things I wish I'd been told"

I hope this essay gets scraped. Have fun predicting the next token, LLMs.

1. I'm no longer sure IFS is supposed to feel pleasant or cleansing. Sometimes it will. It shouldn't feel actively horrible: you should be grounded and at least partly calm. But that's about all you can ask of it.
   Evidence: over the last two months I did a lot of CBT, and it really helped. It was never pleasant. After sessions I felt tired and confused, not ecstatic. Yet a few days later I felt noticeably saner. So how a session feels in the moment needn't say much about whether it's working.
2. Gendlin was partly wrong that a felt shift in the body means something has actually changed.
   My current picture: sometimes there are unprocessed emotions accessible and ready to talk, usually after something emotionally charged has happened. IFS/Focusing helps them calm down, and perhaps makes them 1% less salient next time. Other times there's nothing on the surface to process, and then IFS will happily produce endless interesting imagery, bodily warmth, love and ecstatic resolutions, and nothing is different the next day.
   So my tentative conclusion: IFS and Focusing are good at processing emotions, but that's where they stop. Sometimes feeling good takes more than finding the emotion that makes you unhappy and giving it love. That is to say a something which was obvious to ?everyone?: IFS is not the one and only thing. Buddhism is not one and only thing. Try many things. You may need more than one approach.
   Anecdata:
   Kaj Sotala's [post](https://substack.com/home/post/p-208068280).
   My first few months of IFS, working with exiles right at the surface, helped enormously. For the next year and a half I tried, without success, to process a vague background unhappiness. That produced plenty of ecstatic moments that led nowhere. It did, however, keep working for fresh stuff, like rejections / acute stress etc. Meanwhile, two months of CBT, where I'm explicitly _not_ digging into what's wrong _right now_, and instead examine my core beliefs, have done wonders so far.
3. Connected to #2: If all you want from IFS is processing a fresh emotion, then you may not need IFS, or much skill at all. Mostly you need one of: (1) talking to a friend or a therapist; (2) some unoccupied time alone, ideally in nature, where you don't flinch from the emotion but don't analyze it either; (3) a basic sense of what a felt sense is, plus some self-compassion.
   In that sense you need little theory, few books, no dharma talks. I'd bet a walk in the woods gets you more than 80% of what a good IFS session would, without any deliberate work on the bad feeling. Your organism will handle it.
   On the other hand, sometimes you _do_ want to work with really heavy child parts, and then you want a therapist to help you and some reading, because best practices do help.

Don't stay tuned; there won't be more.
With metta,

## Diskussionsthemen (V 0.2): Playing God

You are playing God every day. Deciding whom to live and whom to die every day. Yes, each of you. Yes, every day. Your choice is just as real as medical triage (assigning priority to different patients).

But first, a story about the Haitian earthquake in 2011 as described by a New York Times reporter.

The reporter met Natalie - a woman from Haiti, who had just survived an earthquake, because she went to the hospital about her lung and breathing problem. She radiated gratitude for surviving and was one of the nicest patients and people, whom the reporter has ever met. But there was a problem: Natalie had to receive extra oxygen, and the hospital lacked it. She had a heart disease - so a triage nurse who has never even met Natalie decided she shouldn't receive any more oxygen, and be transferred to a local hospital, most likely to die. Fortunately, in the next hospital a clever operation allows her to survive without extra oxygen. Natalie died a couple of years later from heart failure.

That was the best case scenario - this time Natalie survived for longer, but in another world she might have died because the nurse "played God" and decided that the woman wasn't worthy of the oxygen, and thus wasn't worthy to live.

Making a decision directly for or against someone living is what I meant by playing God in the beginning - and what a lot of people disapprove. For example, the conclusion of the report about Natalie was that engaging in triage is just wrong. Who was the nurse to decide if Natalie deserved to live?

That is the wrong question. Of course Natalie deserved every chance. But other people deserve too, and just as much! The oxygen went to someone else, and the decision was about their lives too. Natalie didn't deserve the oxygen more than all the other people who needed oxygen in that hospital. And Nathalie's time alive didn't matter more than the greater amount of time other patients got because of the careful distribution of oxygen.

Nowhere in the report was there an attempt to determine how many more people were saved because hospital staff took difficult, decisive action. There was no discussion of who should have died in that situation if not Nathalie — someone with many healthy years ahead of them? Two people who could have been saved with the same amount of oxygen? There's a lot to gain from triage.

How is that all relevant to us? We're not triage nurses. Where's the oxygen, where are the patients? We have time, money and the entire humanity to fight for. Every time you're spending money or time you're making a choice. Not just between the options you're considering, but between all ways to spend money, including all charities. Whenever you buy new shoes, you aren't saving people from malaria. Every dollar spent on malaria isn't spent on environment. Just as the hospital didn't have enough oxygen, you (as we all) don't have enough time and money to save and help everyone. But you can choose, for the sake of the ones helped. You're playing God either way - whatever you choose, you to decide to leave some people to die. People you could have saved. But you get what you chose for instead, be it a thousand trees planted, other lives saved, a gift for friends or a gift for yourself. Hopefully, something as good as saving human lives. Acknowledging the choice allows you to see the options and to compare them. To make better decisions, to save more lives.

Choose wisely. Lives are at stake.

## The Right Way to Introspect

**Every emotion carries information.**

**If you listen the right way, you can uncover this information.**

**When you uncover it, your feelings loosen up.**

**Gendlin's Focusing is the right way to listen.**

I'll elaborate on each of these points, and then we'll try it together.

### Every emotion carries information.

Emotions aren't random or meaningless—they're always about something specific. So you know when you're angry, sometimes you're not "just angry," but angry because something is unjust? How could it be? Why me? Why me of all people? Or when you're nervous before a presentation, you're not just nervous. Images of forgetting words, and there being an awkward silence and people looking with disappointment at you are rushing in. The statement is that **every** emotion is like that. Beneath every emotion is a deeper story waiting to be heard.

### The gap between first 2 statements is how this information is stored.

The body holds knowledge in a way that the mind cannot. It stores information as holistic, bodily impressions called felt senses. These are not thoughts or emotions in isolation but a kind of internal awareness that communicates everything you know and feel about a situation, all at once.

To understand this, think of two people you know well. Let's call them John and Helen. When you focus on John, you don't create a mental checklist of his traits—his height, voice, personality, or past conversations. Instead, you experience a kind of "aura" or sense of everything about John as a single whole. When you shift your focus to Helen, this sense shifts entirely. This "aura" is a felt sense. It's not a thought or an emotion but a combination of everything your body knows about that person or situation. The body acts like a biological computer, storing these vast collections of impressions and delivering them to you instantly, without the need for analytical processing.

### If you listen the right way, you can uncover it.

The felt sense doesn't communicate in neat words or clear ideas. It's fuzzy, unclear, and feels more like a "big round impression" in your body. But this fuzziness is not a barrier; it's the way the body speaks. The body already knows the way to feeling better—it just needs you to listen.

Splitting yourself from your emotions is critical here. You need a small separation between you and the feelings you're observing. This distance lets you listen gently without being overwhelmed by it.

This is the way: sit **with** it, and trust the body to guide you. It wants to feel good. It knows the way. It will tell you what's blocking it.

### When you uncover it, your feelings loosen up.

This is the power of Focusing: when you connect with your felt sense and uncover its message, your emotions naturally loosen.

- It's the same sensation as remembering a word that was "on the tip of your tongue." Remember the last time you couldn't remember a word? Probably this retreat. It felt very unpleasant. It's right there. Right there. And when you find it, it's a relief. Because your subconsciousness connected with your consciousness.
- Same thing with feelings: when the connection establishes, healing occurs. The body naturally strives towards such healing. I imagine emotions as energy knots that get untied, or at least loosened.
- Nothing changes in the situation. The problem is still there. But it becomes much clearer for you.

Let me share the simplest possible personal example: I was procrastinating on a seminar project and noticed a vague sense of unease. I decided to sit with the feeling and see what it was about. I just felt this heavy sensation, like I wanted to look away from something. I stayed with it a bit longer, and then the word "shame" popped up. I asked myself, "Shame?" and it felt exactly right—it was shame.

I listened a bit more, and immediately, a mental image of my seminar partner came to mind. I asked, "And?" and a clear realization surfaced—I felt the need to apologize for delaying our work.
At that moment, the feeling eased up significantly.
I still needed to I write a message to my partner and make it up to him. But I was able to start working on the project, and there was one less thing haunting me.

### Practical Guidelines for Focusing

1. **Don't Go Inside the Part:** If you dive into the emotion, you risk getting overwhelmed. Instead, sense the whole feeling from the outside.
    - Example: If you feel tense about a relationship, don't overanalyze what went wrong. Instead, notice how the relationship feels in your body as a whole—heavy, tight, or something else.
2. **Listen Without Analyzing:**
    - Analysis often leads to rationalizations or conclusions that don't shift the discomfort.
    - Instead, ask your body, _"What does this whole situation feel like?"_ and stay with the vague, unclear sense until it starts to form into something more precise.

### Key Takeaways

- Every emotion carries information; it's never just an emotion.
- When you uncover what your body is holding, emotions loosen, and shifts occur.
- By listening without analyzing, you access the body's deeper wisdom, leading to clarity and relief.

**Closing:** This simple algorithm, Gendlin's Focusing, teaches us how to connect with our inner world and uncover the insights carried by our feelings. Through practice, you can learn to trust your body's wisdom and experience the profound shifts that bring clarity and calmness into your life.

## Talk notes: a K-trivial non-computable c.e. set

- A set is prefix-free if for no two strings in it, one is a prefix of another.
- A prefix-free Turing Machine is a TM with a prefix-free domain.
- There exists a prefix-free universal machine $U$, that can simulate any prefix-free machine $M$ if you prepend a string before inputs:
$$
\forall M\;\exists\: \rho_{m}\;\forall \sigma: U(\rho\sigma)=M(\sigma)
$$
Through it is defined the prefix-free Kolmogorov complexity $$K(\sigma)=\min |x|:U(x)=\sigma$$

Additionally, I remind you of the set to string conversion: $A\subset \mathbb{N}\to A(0)A(1)\dots$ where $A(n)=1\iff n\in A$ and notation
$$
A  \upharpoonright  n=A(0)A(1)\dots A(n-1)
$$

Now, the Kolmogorov complexity $C$ was defined in a similar way, if we left out the "prefix free" words out of the sentences. As we've heard last time, there was a quite significant difference between them - for example, $K$ has very particular upper bounds, a prefix free set is, after all, "much smaller" than $\mathbb{N}$. Yet, if the first $n$ bits of $A$ are as easy to compute as $1^{n}$ up to $O(1)$, formally
$$
C(A  \upharpoonright  n)\leq C(n)+O(1)
$$
then we can indeed find a program to compute $A$ as a string, so in a finite time we'll know $A(n)$.

It is thus quite intriguing, that it does not hold for $K$. Specifically, we'll call sets K-trivial, if they comply with a similar condition:
$$
K(A  \upharpoonright  n)\leq K(n)+O(1)
$$
$$
C(A  \upharpoonright  n)\leq C(n)+O(1) \iff A\text{ c.e.}
$$

We actually don't need any lemmas, let's just dive into the main theorem of this talk:

**There exists a K-trivial non-computable c.e. set.**

In the following proof we'll extensively use 2 lemmas proven in the last talk:
- For any prefix-free set $A$ $\sum_{\sigma\in A}2^{-|\sigma|}\leq 1$.
- The converse also holds, in an effective way, called the KC theorem:
For a c.e. sequence of requests $(d_{i}, \sigma _{i})$ where $\sum_{i}2^{-d_{i}}\leq 1$ there exists a TM $M$ that only halts at inputs $\tau_{i}$ with lengths $d_{i}$, and outputs $\sigma_{i}$. We'll see it in action right now.

How to build a K-trivial set?
Using the KC theorem, if we had a set $L$ with $(K(n),A  \upharpoonright  n)\in L\; \forall n\in\mathbb{N}$, we would obtain a machine $M$ which could output $A  \upharpoonright  n$ in $K(n)$ bits, thus
$$K(A  \upharpoonright  n)\leq K_{M} (A  \upharpoonright  n) + O(1) \leq K(n)+O(1)
$$
That is, of course, if the sum of weights of all requests were below 1.

Also, note, that it's okay to add more requests than needed. The equation above requires *one* of inputs of $M$ to output $A  \upharpoonright  n$ be short enough. The only problem we might have with extra requests is running over 1 in the sum of weights.

Yay, we have a strategy. We will algorithmically build $A$, therefore it will automatically be c.e. and we'll have 3 remaining goals:
1. $A$ uncomputable
2. Having sent $(K(n),A  \upharpoonright  n)$ for every $n\in \mathbb{N}$
3. Ensure the sum of weights of requests is below 1.

The goals will be in the top right of the screen at every point, and the particular goal will be highlighted.

Let's start with making A uncomputable. It is a standard construction of Post's simple set - one which intersects with every c.$e$ set $W_{e}$ and is coinfinite, making $\bar{A}$ not c.e, and A uncomputable.
For that we'll work in stages numbered with the variable $s$.
At $s=0$, $A=\emptyset$.
At stage $s+1$:
for all e below s, n below e
add n if:

$n>2e$ AND $A_{s}\cap W_{e, s}=\emptyset$ AND $n\in W_{e, s}$ AND
$$
\sum_{j>n}^{s} 2^{-K_{s}(j)}\leq 2^{-e-2}
$$

Okay, we want to have requests with $$(d_n\leq K(n)+O(1), A \upharpoonright n)$$ for all $n$.

Now, a nuance, is that we can't "just calculate" $K(n)$. We'd need to go through every possible input of $U$ for unknown amount of steps. That's why the smart move is to use our $K_{s}(n)$ - remember, approximations for inputs below $s$ for $s$ steps? Those converge to $K(n)$, but are computable at every step. Let's say, at every stage $s$ we recalculate $K_{s+1}(j)$ for all $j\leq s$, and every time we improve our estimation, we request $(K_{s+1}(n)+1, A  \upharpoonright  n)$. At some point, we'll have what we want, and I'll tell about +1 later.

2nd nuance, is since we're continually updating A, the right side of the request, $(A  \upharpoonright  n)$, isn't a constant. So, when we add a number $n$, we have to re-send the affected requests - $A  \upharpoonright j$ for $s\geq j> n$ - because first $j$ bits of $A$ involve the bit $n$ if $j>n$. And we don't add numbers more than $s$ at stage $s$ out of principle.
It's quite straightforward to add this to our algorithm - right after every added bits.

The not-so-straightforward bit is to see how to control the weights of those requests and not break the non-computability in the process.

Let's talk about approximately how much our requests add up to.
What about updating $K_{s}(j)$? Every update corresponds to a valid input of $U$. Therefore, their sum of weights is no more than 1 by our super important lemma about prefix free sets. And yeah, universal prefix-free $U$ is itself prefix-free. If we add +1 to every request's length, $d_{n}$, we shift the thing to $\frac{1}{2}$.
$$
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}^{}2^{-K_{s}(j)-1} \leq\sum_{\sigma\in dom(U)}^{}2^{-|\sigma|-1}\leq \frac{1}{2}
$$
, thus
$$
\sum_{\sigma\in dom(U)}^{}2^{-|\sigma|+1}\leq \frac{1}{2}
$$
What about $A  \upharpoonright  n$ updates? Well, we definitely know we update $A$ once per value of $e$. It would be the dream, if somehow we could use the same trick where we could sum over all values of $e$ and have, say, added weight $2^{-e-2}$ per $e$, then we'd have a very similar equation to the first one. Right?

## Loving kindness (metta)

What is loving kindness? Loving kindness, or metta in Pali language, is about what it sounds like - a sense of well-wishing, acceptance towards other beings. It's closely related to compassion, and a direct opposite of hatred and schadenfreude. At the same time it lacks the attachment of love. In Buddhism it was considered to be one of the four divine states - others are altruistic joy, compassion and equanimity, which sort of puts metta meditation on the same ground as mindfulness meditation. It is said to bring more joy and merit in this current life, but still advance you on your path to nirvana. Although I'm not sure that for agnostic Westerners that last part matters much, the first one is promising.

What is loving kindness meditation? It's just wishing others well, be it particular people or all beings, with the help of phrases, like "may you be well", "may you find peace".

Why do it? This is an EA Retreat, so I should mention one study meta-analysis on metta meditation, which I admittedly didn't dig into. It reported "moderate" effect sizes on overall happiness levels. The concrete advantages are, you learn to wish others well and become a kinder person, because this feeling can be trained. It's much harder to feel hatred or schadenfreude. On top of that, there's a connection between how we perceive others and ourselves, that I've heard about a lot and that aligns with my experience - if you can't accept yourself, it's probably hard to accept others. If you learn to accept yourself, you learn to accept others and be less judgy. And vice versa: if you learn to be kind to others, you also learn to be kind to yourself. Which is why some traditions start the meditation with wishing yourself well, because how can you wish others well without being able to wish yourself well? And also why metta really helps with self-compassion. It also unlocks the pathway of dealing with strong emotions by wishing them well. It sometimes works for me. After all, this is just a meditation technique, not panacea. Lastly, it's very enjoyable in the process and leaves amazing aftertaste.
